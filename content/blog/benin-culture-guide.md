Understanding a few cultural basics goes a long way in Bénin, where warmth and formality coexist more than visitors sometimes expect.

## Languages

French is the official language, used in government, business and education. Alongside it, Fon, Yoruba, Goun, Bariba and dozens of other local languages are spoken across the country's regions. English is spoken in some tourist-facing businesses but not widely — a private guide bridges this comfortably.

## Greetings matter

Greetings are taken seriously in Bénin. Skipping a greeting to get straight to a question or request can come across as abrupt. A simple "Bonjour" and a moment of small talk before business, even a quick purchase, is the norm and genuinely appreciated.

## Dress

Bénin is a relatively conservative, warm-climate country. Light, breathable clothing is practical, but modest coverage — particularly at religious sites, temples and in rural areas — is respectful and expected. Swimwear is fine at the beach, not elsewhere.

## Religion and spirituality

Bénin is religiously diverse — Christianity, Islam, and Vodun (the indigenous spiritual tradition, officially recognised and nationally celebrated) all coexist, often within the same extended family. Vodun in particular deserves genuine respect rather than curiosity treated as spectacle; <a href="/vodun-culture.html">our Vodun culture guide</a> goes into this in depth.

## Photography

Always ask before photographing people, especially at markets, ceremonies, or in villages. Most people are happy to be photographed if asked; being photographed without permission is a common and understandable source of friction.

## Hospitality

Beninese hospitality is genuine and often striking to first-time visitors — being invited into someone's home, offered food, or welcomed into a family celebration is not unusual, particularly outside the largest cities. Accepting graciously, even briefly, is generally the right response.

## Tipping

Tipping isn't as deeply embedded a custom as in some countries, but it's appreciated for good service — restaurants, drivers, and guides. Rounding up or adding roughly 10% is a reasonable guide.

## Bargaining

At markets, some negotiation on price is normal and expected, done with good humour rather than aggression. Fixed-price shops and restaurants don't involve bargaining.

A knowledgeable local guide does more than handle logistics — they translate context you'd otherwise miss entirely, from why a greeting matters to what's actually happening at a ceremony you're witnessing.
